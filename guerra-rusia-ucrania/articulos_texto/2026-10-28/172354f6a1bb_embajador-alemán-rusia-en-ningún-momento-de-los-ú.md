## Texto Original (EN):

### German Ambassador: Russia has at no time in the past four and a half years negotiated in a serious way

Boris Ruge has been Germany’s new ambassador to Ukraine since August.Ukraine is not new to him, though. Until recently Ruge was a senior NATO official, serving as Assistant Secretary General for Political Affairs and Security Policy – essentially the third-highest position in the Alliance’s civilian hierarchy. And, of course, he was closely involved with Ukraine and supported closer ties for Ukraine with the Alliance.Now Ruge speaks on behalf of Berlin.And when he tells European Pravda in this interview that Germany supports NATO membership for Ukraine, that represents a clear German position. But his statements on EU membership for Ukraine are even clearer and more detailed.This and much more in the ambassador’s first interview in office.
"We are absolutely determined to stay here"In your view, what does Ukraine mean to Germany?Ukraine is a very important partner for Germany – in technical terms we could say a strategic partner, as we agreed in the spring of this year. We understand how important Ukraine is for European security, and we also understand its weight as a major economic player.You’ve worked at NATO, so you’ll know that pretty much every member is described as "a highly valued ally". It’s the same for Ukraine, isn’t it?No, it’s not the same for Ukraine. Ukraine is a big country, and it is, unfortunately, at the heart of the largest war in Europe since 1945, which has been going on for more than four years now – tragically so. The outcome of this war will determine Europe’s future security order.As far as we are concerned, Europe’s future security order should include a Ukraine that is sovereign, independent and democratic – and able to move towards and join the European Union, certainly. And NATO as well: we said a few years ago that the future of Ukraine is in NATO.All of these things matter. At the core is a fundamental point: Ukraine must be able to exercise its own choices and be part of what, in the old days, we would have called the free world. And I think that is very relevant today – if we look at what Russia represents today, it is definitely not the free world.So, NATO membership, EU membership, and a democratic Ukraine. Those are encouraging messages at the start of our interview, and we’ll come back to each of them later. But first let me say that I’m particularly happy to see you here in Kyiv after last night’s missile attack [the interview took place on 22 October]. Do you know why I say that?No.Because we Ukrainians remember 2022, when one embassy after another was leaving Kyiv. Can we be certain that this won’t happen again?Yes. I think you can be absolutely sure. You remember that Chancellor [Friedrich Merz] came to Kyiv on 4 October. And on 3 October, we were told [by Moscow] that all foreigners should leave Kyiv because it was dangerous and we risked losing our lives by staying here.But the Chancellor was not deterred from coming here.We are here. We know that the coming months will be difficult – over the past two months, we have already seen the security situation become more difficult. We need to handle that, and we need to look after the security of our people, but we are absolutely determined to stay here. It’s the same for all our European and NATO partners.
"EU membership won’t happen in 2027 or 2028. That's impossible. It's not doable"You’ve already said that you see Ukraine as a member of the EU. But is that goal achievable within a reasonable timeframe? And what exactly is a "reasonable timeframe"?That’s a good question. As we all know, to become a member of the European Union, a country has to adapt its legislation, regulations, institutions and so on. That’s not arbitrary, and it’s not something we’ve made up to make things complicated. It’s simply that the European Union is so highly integrated and demands so much of its members. Ukraine needs to go through those steps – and doing so will also make Ukraine a more European country in a broader sense.I don’t think you will find a government in the European Union that is more forward-leaning on EU membership than Germany. I remember that you interviewed my predecessor, Heiko Thoms, about the letter that Chancellor Merz wrote back in May [on a special format for Ukraine’s integration into the EU].Among other things, the letter called for full membership for Ukraine – I think it says so three times in a single letter – and, basically, for opening all negotiation clusters now.And it says that, as Ukraine moves towards the European Union, Ukrainians – representatives of Ukraine – should be brought into the institutions: the Commission, the Parliament, the Council, the European Court of Justice. It’s a very forward-leaning, very positive vision of how we see Ukraine and of our wish for Ukraine to advance rapidly.How long it will take is a very difficult question.I think it's a good thing thatpeople here in Kyiv have moved away from the notion that it will happen in 2027 or 2028.It will not happen then. It’s impossible. It’s not doable.We’re now hearing hopes that Ukraine could join by 2030, and this is being discussed in Brussels as well, not just in Kyiv. Is that an achievable goal, even if it is an ambitious one?2030 is ambitious. First of all, it depends on how Ukraine performs. And then, of course, the reality is that it’s also a very political process, because there are 27 members of the European Union and they all have a say. So it’s difficult to predict, but I think the geopolitical case for Ukraine joining the European Union is very strong.
What matters is that, together, we keep Ukraine on track, moving forward as fast as possible towards full membership – so that people in this country can feel that this is something they can work towards, and that they have a real opportunity to become a member of the European Union within a reasonable time.Is it theoretically possible for Ukraine to join the EU while the war is still ongoing?I don't know whether the European Union has taken a position on whether a continuation of the war would stand in the way of Ukraine becoming a full member. I don’t know that. I don’t believe that’s the case.We should work with Ukraine, even under these very difficult circumstances, to move forward on the path to the European Union. And then we’ll take it from there."There's an understanding, but I remind people that this is the case"The path to EU membership is first and foremost about reforms.Last week, several laws were passed that are relevant to the European Union and to Ukraine’s membership – including the law on minorities, but many others as well. There’s a whole list of things – I think they’re very well known to the Office of the President, to the government, to the Rada [Ukrainian parliament] – that need to be done. And in some cases, draft laws or executive decisions are relevant to unlocking funding from the European Union for Ukraine, which is hugely important.And we keep reminding people that, given the overall situation, we can only encourage Ukraine’s institutions to adopt this legislation as soon as possible, so that the funding the European Union has available for Ukraine can be released.Do you think the Ukrainian government and our MPs understand that all the reforms must be implemented? That cherry-picking reforms is not an option?I think there’s an understanding, but I can tell you that I do remind people that this is the case. To raise the very significant resources – the massive resources – needed to support Ukraine and help it defend itself, we also need to be able to tell people in the German parliament, the Bundestag, in the European Parliament and in the European Council that Ukraine is doing its part and delivering on the reform agenda.Is Ukraine making tangible progress on the rule of law and the fight against corruption?I mean, there are issues with corruption. There were very prominent cases. But at the same time, we can see that Ukraine has institutions, created after the Revolution of Dignity, that are able to investigate and prosecute people even in very high positions. That’s pretty significant. Maybe it’s not perfect, but nothing ever is.There is real action on anti-corruption, and that’s extremely important because – again, in Germany – to make the case for supporting Ukraine, there has to be a credible anti-corruption architecture in place and credible action against people who misuse funds for their own purposes.
I remember interviewing German and other European ambassadors five or ten years ago, and even earlier. Time and again they would describe how corruption was hurting their investors. Is that still the case today?I don’t think that is the main problem for German companies today. And that’s good."Russia has at no time in the past four and a half years negotiated in a serious way"I’d like to ask you about the war, but first, let’s talk about what’s happening around these so-called peace negotiations. I think the best word to describe it is a mess. Would you describe it differently?No, I don’t have another word for it. I think everyone, first and foremost the people in Ukraine, would like to see the war come to an end – after four and a half years of full-scale war and, before that, eight years of a bloody war at a lower level.I can see that there is an American-led effort to bring the war to an end. But I would also say, as a German diplomat, that it is clear that Europe has to be part of the picture. The first point is that Ukraine and the people of Ukraine have to decide on what basis they are willing to end the war.We are grateful to America for trying to bring the war to an end. But Europe – and Germany too – certainly has a big stake in how this war ends, and so we will be there and find a way of articulating our European interests and working with Ukraine to make sure that we end up with a result that is acceptable.Is Europe playing a sufficiently meaningful role in the process right now?What we can see is that, as you remember, the national security advisors of France, Britain and Germany have been to Kyiv twice most recently in September. We also had some national security advisors in Oslo just now. So there is a presence, recently in Miami as well.At the same time, we’re hearing criticism from Poland, our closest EU neighbour. Polish officials don’t hide their frustration that they’re never invited to these discussions. What’s the reason for that?I don’t know whether it’s true that they’re never invited. But Poland is clearly very important. It’s an important member state of the European Union and an important ally in NATO. It is a neighbour, and it is very much impacted by this war. So I think Poland’s desire to be closely involved is important, clearly. I think there are questions about what the formula will ultimately be.
In a sense, we could say that we have not yet entered into a proper negotiation process. And if we’re serious, we can also say that the Russian Federation has at no time in the past four and a half years negotiated in a serious way. They have always stuck to their basic demands, which essentially amount to Ukraine capitulating – so that is not a negotiation. So I would say we’re not yet in a real negotiation process, and we will see what format it takes.So for now, as far as I understand, you don't see Russia being ready for serious steps.Exactly. I mean, last night [21 October] is a demonstration that Russia talks in very vague terms about its willingness to engage, to meet and so on – and in the meantime it attacks Ukraine with its jet drones and ballistic missiles, killing civilians, attacking civilian targets and committing war crimes. So that’s not exactly the mindset of peace. I think we have to be very clear about that.There was a change in the Ukrainian position beginning in 2025, when the president signalled that he would be willing to accept a ceasefire – a full ceasefire. Since then, we have been talking about the Black Sea deals, grain deals and energy ceasefires – all sorts of things. Russia has rejected every single one of them, and I think the record is perfectly clear."I wouldn't say NATO is stepping back. Part of the response is not visible"Is there a risk that Russian attacks could extend beyond Ukraine?Russia’s strategy consists of fighting on land. But the front line is relatively stable. Ukraine has done quite a good job of slowing Russia down and, obviously, of retaking territory recently as well.There’s a war happening in the air, with cruise missiles and drones, where Russia is unfortunately having an impact on the energy infrastructure and attacking the economy – the steel industry, for example. And then there’s a third element: Russia’s hybrid campaign against NATO and EU states, which has been going on for years. Russia is not engaging in a military conflict with NATO, but it is pushing the limits of its hybrid campaign.In my view, when an explosive-laden drone attacks Leipzig Airport, that’s no longer a hybrid attack. It’s more like a conventional attack, isn’t it?In the case of Leipzig, Germany made an attribution. We did our homework: we didn’t go out the next morning and say it was Russia – our intelligence services did their homework, and then we attributed it to Russia. And then we took a number of measures: we closed the consulate in Bonn, we closed the Russian House in Berlin, and we did a number of other things to push back.But you still consider it a hybrid attack?You know, people argue about hybrid, and I accept that hybrid is perhaps a problematic term. You could say it’s grey-zone – a kind of grey-zone campaign that they’re waging.But Russia is not engaged in a large-scale military campaign against NATO. And why is that? Because NATO’s deterrence against Russia has worked. So they have been trying to operate below the threshold of an actual military attack.They've been testing us. And we have to find ways of pushing back.I was in Berlin at the beginning of September for our ambassadors’ conference. Mark Rutte was there to speak, and he said – and I agree entirely – that if Russia engages in these hybrid operations, the response should be more support for Ukraine, not less, because Russia’s objective is to undermine our support for Ukraine.And you could see it when Chancellor Merz came here on 4 October with a package of one billion [euros] in military support for Ukraine. So maybe people in Moscow should register that, despite Leipzig and everything else that has been going on against EU states, NATO states and Germany, our support for Ukraine has not gone down. It has gone up.
And yet I’m seeing attacks that ten years ago would have been considered conventional acts of military aggression – Russia is striking NATO member states with drones, targeting Turkish ships, and sinking ships in the territorial waters of Bulgaria or Romania. And nothing happens! There’s no response from the Alliance. So Russia keeps raising the stakes, and NATO is stepping back.I wouldn’t say NATO is stepping back, but I can tell you that I’ve talked to many Ukrainian colleagues and I know that this is the perception here: that Russia is pushing and NATO has not come up with a strong response. I think part of the response is not visible. But I’ve been telling colleagues from NATO, my former colleagues, that this is the perception here and that they should think about it: The impression that NATO is not responding strongly enough to Russia’s campaign. I think that’s a point people should reflect on in Brussels.                                                     "Germany wants Ukraine to be as close to NATO as possible"I’m glad to hear that you see Ukraine as a future NATO member. But there’s one opponent to us joining, and that’s the United States. Do you think Ukraine will join the Alliance despite that opposition?For Ukraine to join NATO, you need all 32 NATO allies to agree. And if you look at the procedure, there are actually several steps where 32 ambassadors need to raise their hand and say "Yes, we agree."What we said in Washington – and that is the NATO position, as Mark Rutte has reminded people – was that the future of Ukraine is in NATO and that Ukraine is on a… I can’t come up with the adjective right now. Незворотній is the Ukrainian term.Yeah, absolutely! Незворотній.Irreversible! Sorry.So you know the Ukrainian word better!:)Exactly – so I do remember only the Ukrainian word.
It’s on an irreversible path to full Euro-Atlantic integration. When exactly that will happen is, again, a political issue. The difference today, at least from a German point of view, is that it’s very clear that Ukraine is not just the recipient of our assistance, but also a major element of Europe’s defence today.We need Ukraine, with its armed forces and its expertise in modern warfare, to be part of our defence against a very aggressive and imperialist Russia whose ambitions go beyond Ukraine. That is our analysis.What is your vision for Europe’s future security architecture, and what role would Ukraine play in it?In my view, it revolves around NATO. I’m a former NATO person, so maybe that’s logical. But NATO is the organisation in charge of collective defence. I think the role that the United States plays in NATO is changing – we can see that – because of the change in US force posture globally. As you know, that has been going on for 20 years: Barack Obama talked about the pivot to Asia, and that was in the years after 2008. And now China has become even stronger, and the US is required to play its balancing role in the Indo-Pacific.So Europeans will have to step up and play more of a role. But to my mind, Ukraine is already very, very close to NATO. We have the NATO-Ukraine Council, and we do a lot of things together. We have the Joint Analysis Training and Education Centre in Bydgoszcz, Poland, and we are learning from Ukraine today when it comes to unmanned systems.We don’t know when Ukraine will be able to join NATO as a full member, but, from a German point of view, we want Ukraine to be as close to NATO as possible, because in reality you are already part of our collective defence.What are the key elements of a peace settlement that is acceptable to Europe? And please don’t just say that it’s for Ukraine to decide. You have your own position too, don’t you?Sure. Our position is that Ukraine should survive as a sovereign, independent, democratic nation that can make its own choices.What about other conditions, particularly concerning Russia? Some people say that to secure a lasting peace, Europe would need to restore ties with Russia and resume supplies of Russian oil and gas through Ukraine. What’s your view on that?I don’t believe that’s the case. If you look at the German-Russian relationship after 1991, you could say two things.One is that Germany got it terribly wrong. We were wrong to assume that we could develop a partnership with Russia. Russia walked away from the partnership we tried to establish and turned into an aggressive, imperialist actor that is trying to overturn the entire political order of Europe. That’s one way of looking at it, and I think in many ways it’s accurate.Another way of looking at it is that Germany and the rest of Europe tried very hard to build a constructive and positive relationship with Russia. You can see it in the NATO-Russia Council, and you can see it in the way we developed the energy partnership with Russia, and so on. So we tried, and it failed.We made an honest effort to have a positive, forward-looking relationship with Russia, and Russia threw that out of the window and turned to the use of military force, aggression and this hybrid campaign, this manipulation of information and politics.  This attempt of ours was a failure. But what is important is that we sincerely made efforts to build forward-looking relations with Russia. So the people in Europe today, including in my country, Germany, who say we should go back to that and build a positive relationship with Russia have obviously forgotten that we spent 20 years doing exactly that, and it turned out to be a massive failure.And it turned out to be the biggest failure on energy in particular. The idea at the time was that if Russia had a major market in Western Europe, it would have a stake in maintaining peace and building a positive relationship. But that is not what Mr Putin ultimately decided.We are not going back to that. I don’t believe you will find a majority in Germany for that. Maybe people in Washington DC think we should re-establish that energy partnership between Western Europe and Russia, but I think in Western Europe you will find precious few people willing to make themselves dependent on Russia in any way today.Interview by Sergiy SydorenkoVideo by Mykhailo AlekseevEuropean Pravda
