### EU Ambassador: Ukraine could join the EU by 2030, but reforms must accelerate right now

EU Ambassador to Ukraine Katarina Mathernová has been in office since 2023, but she has been working on the issue of Ukraine’s accession to the European Union for well over 10 years.Mathernová has announced that she will be stepping down as ambassador next year and leaving EU institutions altogether, which means that this year’s report assessing Ukraine’s progress on reforms was her last. But the report is special to her for another reason too: she personally played a part in ensuring that it was very positive for Ukraine, confirming the country’s progress, and she stands by that decision.At the same time, she acknowledges that the pace of reforms leaves much to be desired."Ukraine needs to accelerate, and the time to accelerate is now!" the diplomat says emphatically. This is essential if Ukraine is to catch up with the other leading candidate countries, including Moldova.Why is it objectively difficult for Ukraine to implement reforms? How can these challenges be overcome? And what does Mathernová consider a realistic date for Ukraine’s accession? The ambassador answers these and many other questions in this interview with European Pravda."Not a single euro was lost"European ambassadors have been saying in recent weeks that they intend to stay in Kyiv whatever happens. Can we be sure that the EU Delegation will remain here even if the situation in Kyiv deteriorates?We are here and we are staying. We are not yielding to Russian threats, and we are here with Ukraine.If we say that we are the biggest, most reliable and strongest supporter of Ukraine, that's also about being here.
I cannot predict what the situation will be. If there is an Armageddon in public services and everything, then we'll see what the situation is. But you know, last winter was already pretty tough and we were here, and we certainly plan to stay here too. We are making preparations for the winter just like every Ukrainian family.And we will be here.The EU summit in Dublin has just concluded, and Ukraine’s funding gap was also discussed there. What was the key?The issue of the financial gap has been on the table since the very end of August.Then it took a little bit of time to stabilise the figures, but now it's very clear: the gap is foreseen to be around €70 billion next year – 30 billion on the civil side and 40 billion on the military side.Has the EU confirmed this amount?Yes, those are the figures that we are working with. And there are several issues I would like to stress.First of all, the European Union is looking at being able to do roughly two-thirds of this gap, and we expect that other partners of Ukraine will fulfil another one-third. So there is very much an expectation that this is going to be a joint undertaking with like-minded partners.The second point I want to make: we still have two major financial instruments from the European Union that are being implemented. The first one is the European facility, since 2024, that is still disbursing this year and next year. That's the €50-billion programme.And we also have the Ukraine Support Loan of €90 billion that was adopted this year, and it's important that these support measures get fully utilised. Support for civilian needs is tied to the adoption of specific reforms outlined in the Ukraine Plan.And despite all that may appear, and all the drama that sometimes accompanies voting in the Rada [Ukrainian parliament] and so on, I would like to stress that so far, not a single euro was lost.Doing reforms is not physics. So sometimes there were delays, but until now there has not been a single euro lost. The delayed reforms were then done, and I very much expect that we'll continue in this good track record."Ukraine needs to accelerate. And the time to accelerate is now"The implementation of reforms is linked to Ukraine’s European future. The European Commission has assessed these reforms in its Enlargement Report, which was published last week. What is your assessment of this document?It's the last Enlargement Report in my capacity as ambassador to Ukraine, because I started my fourth year. And I'm very happy to say that it's the strongest and most encouraging.In what sense?It's a report that finally – and I very deliberately use the word "finally" – gives Ukraine credit for the cumulative reforms it has undertaken in specific areas.Finally, there was now a recognition in 14 different areas, including virtually all of the Fundamentals, about raising Ukraine up a notch in the level of preparedness.In the report, one matrix looks at reforms in the specific area of "What have you done since last year?" But the level of preparedness looks at cumulative reforms that have taken place – for example, the track record.Building a track record is not easy, and this is where Ukraine is very strong. In anti-corruption, there is a track record: you have independent institutions, and they deliver.All of this was finally taken into account. So I am very happy with the Enlargement Report.As a citizen of Ukraine, I’d like to ask: when will we be ready to join the EU? At the current pace of reforms, as reflected in this report, it will take another 10 years...With all due respect, I think that is perhaps a bit pessimistic.I understand the frustration, and we were also very actively entering the dialogue when there was a slowdown of reforms at the end of last year and the first half of this year.But there is an acceleration of reforms now.And what I also liked about the report is that it's very honest. It gives a very strong push to Ukraine to accelerate, because I very much agree: if the reforms were proceeding at the pace that we saw in the first half of this year, then it would be very difficult to move on the enlargement track.
But look at the performance of the Rada in the last few weeks. Look at the plenary of this week. They passed a number of important reform laws in the first reading, some in the second reading.I do believe that the combination of the enlargement recommendations and the finances under the Ukraine Plan would allow Ukraine to lock step with the rest of the frontrunners next year.But it will need to accelerate. And the time to accelerate is now."Meet the interim benchmarks by next summer, and accession by 2030 is realistic"So what would be a realistic date for meeting all the accession requirements?I personally think – and I’ve mentioned that several times, including publicly – that the end of the decade is realistic to enter the EU.It’s still realistic.That’s quite ambitious.But Ukraine would need to sustain the accelerated mode of reforms, especially the ones that are tied to the interim benchmarks [IBAR], which is the next big issue.That acceleration would need to be sustained – as difficult as it sounds – during wartime. It would require sustained reform momentum and finalising all the technical work by the end of 2028, as is written in the report.And the next important step – the most important step – is to do the interim benchmarks, because that's the basis on which individual chapters can be closed.The President’s Office, the government and you have all said that completing the IBAR is our next goal. When do you think this could realistically happen?The outline and the goal that was set by the Commission in the report, based on Ukrainian planning, is the first half of next year, in the summer.
I know that the prime minister and his team have plans to do it even earlier. I'm hedging a little bit, simply because I expect that the winter will be very, very difficult, and we may have other worries than the interim benchmarks there for a few weeks.But I do think that the summer of next year is realistic.This means that Moldova will be a bit ahead of us, as it will receive its IBAR earlier. Is it a problem if Ukraine and Moldova become decoupled in the accession process?Look, I know the issue of Moldova is extremely sensitive.I don't only read it in the newspaper – I hear it from friends over dinner. So I realise that this is something that people perceive as a matter of fairness, and they are almost ashamed that Moldova is ahead.But let's look at the facts. Yes, there is hybrid warfare in Moldova, just like in the rest of Europe, but they don't have attacks. They don't draft laws in shelters. They are not bombarded on a daily basis. They are not in an acute state of war. That's one element.And the second one: let's look at the situation. Your parliament is in the eighth year of this convocation. There are very few parliaments that would sustain the activity over such a period of time, because normally in a democracy you would change the legislature already – there would be elections, which is impossible during the bombardments and during martial law.And you have a constitutional requirement where you need 226 votes, which is 50% plus one vote, from a theoretical, never-fulfilled number of MPs, because it includes temporarily occupied territories and Crimea.So you suddenly are faced with a situation that every single law, every single action of parliament, requires a 60% majority.Even a bit more.Even a bit more than 60, or at least 60%.You can’t compare it with Moldova's new parliament following recent elections.So I understand the frustration that Moldova is ahead. But for all of us, it's completely natural.The EU usually conducts enlargement in waves. Does Ukraine have a chance of joining the next enlargement wave?Fulfil the interim benchmarks by next summer, and I think then we are in the same game.
"You need to find a way to convince sceptical voices"What are the key things the Rada has to do?For example, there is a law on prosecutors in parliament – it’s passed the first reading and needs to pass the second reading.For the interim benchmarks, there is also the introduction of a more transparent and more merit-based selection of the prosecutor general. That's one pain point.Another complicated one is the reform of the State Bureau of Investigation.Then there are a number of others that relate to the selection of judges, the Constitutional Court, the Supreme Court, the high judiciary bodies, but I think we are on a good track there.And here I would like to mention: in October after the agreement between the two presidents, von der Leyen and Zelenskyy, we established a Task Force, which is politically led by Prime Minister [Serhii] Koretsky and Commissioner [Marta] Kos, but also has a working level where both Deputy Prime Minister Vsevolod Chentsov and Minister of Justice Denys Maslov are very involved. And this Task Force meets twice a week. [The heads of the Task Force met in Brussels on Sunday, after this interview had taken place – ed.]And the whole idea of the Task Force is to help Ukraine deliver on the IBAR sooner.I think that by now there is a very good group dynamic.The benchmarks remain the same. This is just a tool with which to interpret them, understand the interpretation, push, find solutions. I think this is going to be a very useful mechanism.The Task Force has been established between the government and the European Commission. But in practice, MPs will come along and say: Ukraine negotiated the benchmarks poorly, and we are not going to do this or that.That’s a very good point. It was identified very quickly as an issue. And I'm very happy to say that there is now work together with MPs who are responsible for specific areas.So for example, Mrs Radina [Anastasiia Radina, Chair of the Verkhovna Rada Committee on Anti-Corruption Policy] is invited to the Task Force when issues concerning the anti-corruption strategy are discussed.What about Ionushas? [Serhii Ionushas, Chair of the Verkhovna Rada Committee on Law Enforcement, has traditionally been critical of European legislation.]When we are going to be discussing reform of law enforcement agencies, Mr Ionushas will be there as well.And what we are also doing is that we have been reaching out to a broader stakeholdership among civil society. Because voices of one-two-three were mostly heard in these areas, but there is actually a much broader group of civil society actors that work on issues that touch the Fundamentals – whether it's democratic institutions or elections, or anti-corruption, or judiciary.You mentioned Radina, but she needs no convincing of the importance of reforms for EU accession. But when Ionushas is involved, problems arise. It’s no secret that there are sceptical voices in parliament.There are sceptical voices, and I think that one thing that the report says is that EU accession is a whole-society effort. Journalists play an absolutely indispensable role. Civil society plays an indispensable role.You need to find a way to convince sceptical voices.I was in the National Council of Slovakia when they were voting for accession to the EU, and it was the only time ever that we had the full parliament, 100% participation and 100% vote – also the sceptics. There are always going to be sceptics.
"There needs to be some kind of selection process for the prosecutor general"Is it possible to convince the EU that Ukraine should not have to meet certain benchmarks?That's very difficult. But what I think regarding fulfilling the benchmarks: I think the benchmarks were well chosen and are valid. But the way they're drafted, they all offer some space for interpretation and finding solutions, and I think this is where the Task Force is very relevant – to find a way out of something that may not look obvious.Is it going to satisfy every voice of civil society or every individual member [of parliament]? Probably not.But I think we have found ways on judicial selections, we have found ways on the international experts, we have found a good text of the law on prosecutors. Not about the prosecutor general, but the reform of the prosecution, the disciplinary…Does the EU support the current draft law on reforming the prosecution service?Yes, yes! Again, there are voices that say, "Oh, but it doesn't cover this and that."But it was never meant to cover the selection of the prosecutor general. That was never meant to be in this law. It's not in that law, and that's fine for the Ukraine Plan and for receiving the money that is attached to this law. Colleagues are still looking at it, but I haven't heard any major issues.Am I right in understanding that Ukraine needs to amend the procedure for appointing the prosecutor general, and only then can it receive a positive IBAR assessment?Yes, because there is a call for a more transparent and merit-based procedure. That's the language from the interim benchmark.So that means there should be some kind of selection process for candidates?There needs to be some kind of selection, and I think the Task Force is now discussing the various options. And we'll do that also in the new year, because the deadline for this point is not now.But we are also mindful of the constitutional provisions that deal with the prosecutor general and with the powers of the president etc. We are aware of the fact that during martial law you cannot amend the Constitution even if you had the votes.And this is where the Task Force is great – we can find a solution that will satisfy the IBAR, both the spirit and the letter of the IBAR.A few days ago, the Verkhovna Rada passed a long-awaited draft law on SEPA [the Single Euro Payments Area], concerning the establishment of a register of bank accounts and safe deposit boxes. However, MPs reduced the number of authorities that would have access to this register. Is this acceptable to the EU?Our colleagues in Brussels are looking at it, so I don't want to jump the gun here. I understand the concerns about unreformed agencies having access to the registers. But I don't want to prejudge what will happen.There is a scope between first and second reading to do the law in a way that satisfies both the World Bank – because it was one of their requirements – and also alignment with broader EU legislation.SEPA is something similar to visa-free for finances.In a country which is part of the Single Euro Payments Area, it doesn't matter whether I send money to my neighbour in Slovakia, or whether I send money to my son somewhere else, or to a colleague in Italy – it's a domestic transaction.So essentially it unites the European Union, but also countries outside that have joined. And the practical impact would be that the 5 million Ukrainians who live in the European Union and send money to Ukraine or vice versa would not have to pay 50, 60, 70-euro fees. It would be a costless transaction or cost very little.And so it's incredibly important for individual citizens, and it's incredibly important for small businesses in particular."The narrative that Ukraine is the most corrupt country in the world is laughable"Transparency is also important for fighting corruption, so let's talk about that. What are the EU's main expectations and key priorities for Ukraine in this area? What do we need to do now?First and foremost, adopt the Strategy.And then there are several procedural steps – none of them are so sexy, but all are important – in the penal procedure, to make the adjudication faster.We also have the issue of the "Lozovyi amendments" regarding the premature termination of proceedings still on the table. A lot of it was already repealed, but some of it is left – this premature termination of cases.And then we still have the evergreens of enabling NABU and SAPO with qualified forensics capacity that could be used – not that they have the capacity, but that they are able to access independent capacity. But I think that this is something that will be doable to agree with the Ministry of Justice.But here again I want to say something which is very important, and even comparing to the other three frontrunners. Yes, they are further along in the IBARs, for example, through the adoption of legislation. But Ukraine has something which is much harder to come by, and that's the track record.The track record of the whole system, for example, investigating, prosecuting and adjudicating corruption, is very solid in Ukraine. In other places they have the laws, but they don't have the track record. Here you actually have the track record.Every scandal that is discovered is a testimony that the institutions are building a track record, and that they are strong enough to resist any political pressure and can deliver on their mandate.
Does this mean that high-profile corruption scandals can also have a positive side?Of course in the information space they are a problem, because the less you know about Ukraine, the more prone you are to believe various narratives, including Russian narratives, and the Russian narrative on how thoroughly corrupt Ukraine is…… which is not true.I'm convinced it's not true. There is corruption in Ukraine.We have NABU and SAPO discovering corruption, sometimes at shocking levels, in institutions that are supposed to hold up the rule of law.But this is a society that fights corruption!The farcical narrative that it’s the most corrupt country in the world – I mean, that's laughable.At the same time, the Verkhovna Rada can’t even adopt an anti-corruption strategy – MPs are afraid to even consider it. What’s the reason for that? Why are MPs so afraid of anti-corruption legislation?Because you have sceptics, you have institutions that have been built, and people are afraid of what they might discover...Ending up in jail.And ending up in jail.Look, before the Revolution of Dignity there was a system – that is now getting changed, but the transition is never straightforward and never easy."Each new enlargement is more difficult"What can we expect in Ukraine's relations with the EU by the summer of 2027, while you are still serving as ambassador?I very, very much hope that we will have the Interim Benchmarks Report not only drafted but submitted to the Council and well received by member states.This would be a significant step forward on Ukraine's path towards EU membership.Many steps up across many areas, yes.We are making faster progress towards EU membership than NATO membership. Nevertheless, NATO has an officially adopted formula stating that Ukraine is on an "irreversible" path to membership. Why don't we have a similar formula with the EU?Because the member states in the European Union also have their experiences from previous enlargements, and particularly in the area of the judiciary, there were big disappointments.So I think there is, on the contrary, a feeling – and it was part of the 16 October communication that the Commission adopted – there is in fact a desire to put into the accession treaties a sort of stop clause so that in case there are reversals, there would be a way of dealing with it with the new entrance.If you ask me whether each new enlargement is more difficult, I will say absolutely yes. It's more difficult in two aspects. One is that the body of the laws – the amount of acquis communautaire, as we call it – that needs to be taken on board is much bigger than, for example, when we were entering the EU. Because we’ve had the migration crisis, the financial crisis, the banking crisis – and any time that happens, we have EU legislation growing.So the task itself is more difficult. Plus the member states are more wary about reform reversals. And this is not only on the most recent entrance. We see reform reversals also in long-established democracies. And once you are in the bloc, until now there were no mechanisms to really correct that.So I think there will very much be an attempt to put a mechanism like this in place for those who would come.To prevent that from happening.Yes.But still, I believe that Ukraine is on an irreversible path towards EU membership.I completely agree.
